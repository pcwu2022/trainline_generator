Fast Multi-Objective Railway Network Generator: Specification v0.1
Draft for implementation, November 10, 2026. Replaces the annealing/MILP solver of the original railway optimizer with a two-layer design.
1. Purpose and scope
Generate a small set of plausible, internally consistent European railway networks from scratch, in minutes rather than hours.
Required properties:
• Relative realism. Ridership ranks follow real corridors: Paris-Lyon carries far more than Toulon-Perpignan.
• Magnitude realism. Trains per hour, annual passengers, fleet size, capital and operating cost are within roughly a factor of 2-3 of real analogues. A single route must not show a loss of the order of EUR 1B per year.
• Speed. One network evaluation takes milliseconds; a full multi-objective run takes minutes.
• Multi-objective. The output is a Pareto front of networks, not one answer.
• Geography-aware. Track alignment uses terrain, water and population-density tiles.
Non-goals: demand forecasting, timetables, engineering design, brownfield upgrades, freight, cross-border regulation and signalling, political feasibility.
2. Architecture
The problem is split so that expensive geography is computed once and the optimizer only sees abstract links with fixed costs.

4. Stage 0: Preprocessing
4.1 Nodes
Each city is one node with population P_i. Station placement is the cell nearest the city centre (v1). Multiple stations per metro are a v2 extension.
4.2 Station capture and access time
From the population raster, for each node compute:
• kappa_i: share of metro population within the station catchment radius (default 15 km, or center_coefficient * sqrt(density) as in the original model), times an uptake factor p_max (default 0.8-1.0).
• A_i: mean population-weighted access time in hours to the station, using an access speed (default 25 km/h).
These are fixed per node and are the only place where population density enters demand.
4.3 Candidate edges
Build the candidate edge set as the union of:
1. k nearest neighbours of each city (k = 6-8) within d_max (default 600 km).
2. Delaunay triangulation edges within d_max.
3. The top N_force pairs by gravity demand (default 30), even if long, so that major corridors are always available.
Prune an edge A-C when a third city B lies within via_tolerance km (default 15) of the A-C line and d(A,B) + d(B,C) <= 1.05 d(A,C). Such links are represented as A-B and B-C. Also drop edges whose cheapest alignment crosses open sea or exceeds a cost cap.
Optional hub nodes: add up to H candidate junction nodes (default 0-20) at locations where many corridors converge or near large cities, so branches can merge. Hubs have no population and no demand, only transfer capability.
5. Stage 1: Alignment routing on tiles
5.1 Cost surface
For each track type t in {CR (conventional/upgraded), HSR} build a cost raster, in EUR million per km, per cell:
c_t(x) = base_t * terrain_mult(x) * slope_mult_t(x) + water_term(x) + density_term(x)
density_term(x) = c_u * max(0, density(x) - density_ref)
• terrain_mult: flat 1.0, rolling 1.3, hilly 1.8, mountain 3.0 (includes tunnelling). Starting values to verify.
• slope_mult_t: 0-1% grade 1.0, 1-2% 1.2, 2-3% 1.6, 3-4% 2.5; above the grade limit (HSR about 4%, CR about 3%) the cell is blocked.
• water_term: cost per km of crossing, optionally growing with continuous water run length as in the original model. Sea cells are blocked.
• density_term: urban land acquisition and tunnelling premium.
• Blocked cells have infinite cost.
Use separate rasters for CR and HSR because the slope limits and base costs differ.
5.2 Cost-distance routing
For each city node, run a multi-source least-cost sweep on a window of radius d_max around the city. In Python use skimage.graph.MCP_Geometric; on H3 use scipy csgraph Dijkstra. One sweep gives the cost to every tile, so every candidate edge from that city is resolved at once.
For each candidate edge A-B and track type t:
1. Read the cost at B from A's surface. This is the edge capital K_e,t (EUR million).
2. Trace back the path to obtain geometry, tile set and length d_e,t.
3. Smooth the path (Douglas-Peucker followed by Chaikin, tolerance about one cell) and recompute length.
4. Record the share of the path that is water or high-slope, for reporting.
Resolution guidance: 1-2 km cells for the continental extent, with windowed sweeps. With about 100 nodes and 2 track types this is about 200 sweeps of roughly 1-2 s each, so about 5-10 minutes, run once and cached.
5.3 Alternatives
Optional. For selected edges generate one or two alternative alignments by the corridor method (sum of the surface from A and the surface from B, then take a second path after penalizing tiles within a buffer of the first). Alternatives become extra candidate edges with distinct tile sets.
5.4 Output record per edge
edge_id, node_a, node_b, track_type,
length_km, capex_meur, tile_indices (sorted int array),
geometry (polyline), pct_water, pct_steep, feasible (bool)
Persist to disk (parquet and a sparse matrix, see 9.4). Stage 3 never touches the rasters again.
6. Demand model
6.1 Annual OD trips
For each pair of distinct cities (i, j) within max_trip_km (default 1200):
T_ij = k * (P_i * kappa_i)^a * (P_j * kappa_j)^a / d_ij^beta * s(GT_ij)
• d_ij: great-circle distance, floored at d_min (default 30 km).
• a in 0.8-1.0, beta in 1.3-2.0, k fitted (Section 11).
• T_ij is annual one-way trips in the rail-served market, not an hourly rate.
• Pairs not connected by the network have s = 0 and carry nothing.
6.2 Rail share
s(GT) = 1 / (1 + exp((GT - t0) / sigma))
GT_ij = A_i + A_j + t_wait + t_ivt + n_transfers * t_transfer + fare_ij / VOT
• t0 about 3 h and sigma about 0.7 h as starting values: share about 80-90% near 2 h, about 50% near 3 h, under 20% beyond 4-5 h.
• VOT (value of time) about EUR 20-25 per hour; including the fare term makes demand respond to fare level and lets track-type choice matter.
• Optional refinement: let t0 grow with distance to reflect air competition on long pairs.
This replaces hard journey-time cutoffs. Slow routes lose demand smoothly instead of being excluded or forced.
7. Stage 2: Network evaluator
Input is a design x: for each candidate edge a state in {0 = not built, 1 = CR, 2 = HSR}. Where two candidate edges share both endpoints, at most one is active.
7.1 Graph and travel times
1. Build a weighted graph of active edges. Edge in-vehicle time is d_e / v_t + dwell * (stops), with commercial speed v_CR about 120-160 km/h and v_HSR about 200-250 km/h (average including stops).
2. Compute all-pairs shortest in-vehicle paths with scipy.sparse.csgraph.shortest_path (C implementation). For N under 150 this takes about 1-5 ms.
3. Transfers (v1): add t_transfer (default 0.25 h) at any degree-3 or higher node where a path turns by more than 60 degrees. Straight-through running at a node incurs no transfer. A full line-pool model is a v2 option.
7.2 Assignment and frequency (fixed-point loop, at most 3 iterations)
1. Start with f_e = f_init (default 2 trains/hour/direction) on every active edge.
2. Compute t_wait = min(1.0, 0.5 / f) using the lowest frequency along the path (first boarding), then GT_ij, s(GT_ij) and T_ij.
3. All-or-nothing assignment of each T_ij to its shortest path. Accumulate directional annual load L_e on each edge (half of the two-way flow each way, with an optional directional imbalance factor).
4. Update frequency:
   avg_hourly_load = L_e / (days * H_op)
   f_e = clamp(ceil(peak_factor * avg_hourly_load / (S_t * LF)), f_min, f_max)
   Defaults: peak_factor 1.3-1.5, LF 0.60-0.65, f_min 1, f_max 4-6 (track throughput limit).
5. Repeat until no f_e changes or 3 iterations are done.
6. Capacity spill: if load on an edge still exceeds S_t * f_max * LF_max (default LF_max 0.85), scale down every OD flow crossing it by the overload ratio (one pass) and lose the excess trips.
7.3 Fleet, operating cost, revenue
n_trains_in_service = sum_e 2 * f_e * (d_e / v_t + dwell)
fleet = ceil(n_trains_in_service * (1 + turnaround_overhead)) * (1 + spare_ratio)
train_km_per_year = sum_e 2 * f_e * d_e * H_op * days
opex_trains = sum_e c_trainkm_t * train_km_e
revenue = sum_ij T_ij * fare_ij
fare_ij = yield_path * d_ij_GC
yield_path is the distance-weighted mean yield (EUR per passenger-km) of the edges on the path; d_ij_GC is the great-circle OD distance. Fare is based on OD distance, not ridden distance, so detours earn nothing extra. Optional taper: multiply by (d / d_ref)^(-0.15) for long trips.
Default turnaround_overhead 0.15, spare_ratio 0.10.
8. Finance
All values constant-price euros, annualized.
8.1 Capital
• Track: union of tiles over active edges (see 9.4), priced per track type. HSR on a tile overrides CR. Shared tiles are paid once.
• Stations: K_station_i = station_base + station_per_Mpop * P_i (million euros, capped), for every node with at least one active edge.
• Fleet: fleet_HSR * train_cost_HSR + fleet_CR * train_cost_CR.
8.2 Annualization
Capital recovery factor CRF(r, L) = r / (1 - (1 + r)^(-L)), with r about 4% and lifetimes: track 50 y, stations 50 y, fleet 30 y. Annualized capital C_cap = sum_b K_b * CRF(r, L_b). This is about 5% of capital per year for track.
8.3 Recurring cost
C_op = opex_trains + maint_rate * K_track + station_opex_rate * K_station + overhead_rate * opex_trains
C_total = C_cap + C_op
Net = Revenue - C_total          (EUR per year; negative means subsidy needed)
Defaults: maint_rate 1.5% of track capital per year, station_opex_rate 2-3%, overhead_rate 10%.
9. Stage 3: Multi-objective search
9.1 Genome
Integer vector of length |E|, entries in {0, 1, 2} as in Section 7. Optional hub-node genes are implicit: a hub is active if any incident edge is active.
9.2 Objectives (all minimized; negate to maximize)
ID
Objective
Definition
O1
Net annual cost
-(Revenue - C_total) in EUR per year
O2
Passengers
-sum T_ij actually carried, trips per year
O3
Population served
-sum_{i active} P_i * kappa_i
O4 (optional)
Mean generalized time
Trip-weighted mean GT of carried trips
O5 (optional)
CO2 saving
-(avoided emissions) via assumed mode mix
Default run uses O1-O3. O4 is trip-weighted over served trips only, so use it together with O2 to avoid favouring a handful of fast short links.
9.3 Constraints
Handled through the solver's constraint-violation mechanism (not penalties inside objectives):
• Upfront capital <= upfront_budget (0 disables).
• Annual net subsidy <= max_annual_subsidy (0 disables).
• Edge frequency within f_max after spill; fraction of demand lost to spill <= 15%.
• Optional: network connected (largest component share >= L_min). Off by default. Disconnected networks are allowed because coverage floors are exactly what forced loss-making links in the original model.
There are no hard coverage floors. Coverage is an objective (O3), not a constraint.
9.4 Shared infrastructure by tile union
Build a sparse binary matrix M of shape (edges x tiles) per track type from the stored tile sets, plus a per-tile cost vector. For a design:
used_HSR = (M_HSR.T @ (x == 2)) > 0
used_CR = ((M_CR.T @ (x == 1)) > 0) & ~used_HSR
K_track = tile_cost_HSR . used_HSR + tile_cost_CR . used_CR
This is a few sparse matrix-vector products, microseconds to a millisecond. It makes two corridors that share a trunk pay only once, which gives natural junction behaviour without a routing step inside the optimizer.
9.5 Algorithm
Primary: NSGA-II via pymoo with integer variables. Alternatives behind the same interface: NSGA-III or MOEA/D for 4 or more objectives; AMOSA to stay closest to the original annealing moves.
• Population 100-200, generations 100-300, parallel evaluation across cores.
• Crossover: uniform on the edge vector.
• Mutation: per-gene flip (low rate), plus structured mutations: add the cheapest path joining two components, remove the edge with the lowest marginal net value, upgrade CR to HSR on the busiest edge, downgrade HSR on a lightly used edge.
• Repair (Lamarckian): after evaluation, remove active edges with essentially zero load and write the repaired genome back.
• Seeding: 20-30% of the initial population from greedy growth runs (start from the best corridor and add the link with highest marginal net value per euro of capital, repeated at several stopping budgets), the rest random sparse subsets (2-10% of edges active) and subsets biased toward the top demand corridors.
• Termination: generation cap, or hypervolume improvement under a threshold for 20 generations.
9.6 Post-processing
Deduplicate designs, recompute with full precision, and select 5-10 representative designs from the front by k-medoids on normalized objectives. Always include: best net financial result, maximum passengers with Net >= -x for a user-set subsidy x, and the knee point.

11. Calibration and validation
No result is trusted until this stage passes.
11.1 Backtest corridors
Assemble 10-20 corridors with observed annual passengers, deliberately mixing strong and thin ones. Examples to consider: Paris-Lyon, Paris-Brussels, Madrid-Barcelona, Milan-Rome, Berlin-Munich, and thin pairs such as Toulon-Perpignan. Hold out about one third for testing.
11.2 Fitting
Evaluate each corridor on the actual existing line parameters (length, speed, frequency) with the demand function. Fit k (and optionally a, beta, t0) by least squares on the log of ridership over the training corridors. Fix the fitted values for all later runs.
11.3 Acceptance criteria (v1)
Test
Pass condition
Ranking
Spearman correlation of modelled vs observed ridership at least 0.8 on held-out corridors
Anchor magnitude
Median absolute log error at most ln(2) on anchor corridors; none worse than a factor of 3
Strong vs thin
Paris-Lyon class corridor exceeds Toulon-Perpignan class corridor by at least one order of magnitude
Operations
Best corridors show 1-4 trains/hour/direction and a fleet of tens of trainsets, not hundreds
Finance
Best corridors roughly cover operating cost; weak links are rejected by the optimizer, not built
Loss sanity
Flag any single built route with net loss above EUR 200M per year as a probable bug or parameter error
Capex arithmetic
Annualized track capital is about 5% of capital (for example a EUR 8B line needs about EUR 400M per year net revenue to break even)
11.4 Sensitivity
Rerun the front with demand parameters, capex and yield each varied by plus or minus 25%. Report which designs persist across variations.
12. Outputs
• pareto.csv: one row per non-dominated design with O1-O5, capital, fleet, passengers, population served.
• designs/*.geojson: edges with track type, length, capex, load, frequency; nodes with population and served flag.
• report.md or report.html: front plot, selected representative designs, per-edge table, backtest results.
• run_config.json: full parameters and random seed for reproducibility.
• Map plot per representative design.

15. Risks and open questions
• Demand calibration dominates everything. Poor or inconsistent ridership data will bias all outputs. Choose one consistent source set per country.
• All-or-nothing assignment ignores route choice and congestion. The capacity spill step is crude.
• Station-level realism. One station per city ignores multi-station metros and peripheral stations.
• Sea and border crossings (Channel, Scandinavia, Strait of Gibraltar) need explicit hand-built edges and cost overrides.
• Greenfield assumption remains. Without existing track, thin and mid-size corridors will rarely pay back; consider a CR-discount option to approximate partial reuse.
• Objective gaming. Check that no objective can be improved by serving only a few very short or very fast links. O4 should always be paired with O2.
• Search coverage. NSGA-II may miss parts of the front with 100+ edges; compare with a weight-sweep greedy baseline.
• Parameter provenance. Every constant in Section 10 needs a source before publication use.
